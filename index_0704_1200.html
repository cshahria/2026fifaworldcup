Final Shift — Gate Duty, a Megaphone, and a Sold-Out Send-Off

July 1st was it — the last shift at San Francisco Bay Area Stadium. I got there earlier than planned, around 10:30 PT in time to catch the end of England vs. DR Congo at the Hyatt Regency across the street, which was open to the public and packed with good vibes from both Bosnian and American fans. England came back from a goal down, with Kane scoring twice to seal it. Since kickoff for USA vs. Bosnia wasn't until 17:00 — one of the earliest starts of the tournament — I grabbed lunch first, knowing dinner would come at the tail end of the shift. By all accounts this was a sellout at the stadium.

On the way to the volunteer center I caught up with Adrian, who'd visited San Pedro Square the day before for Mexico vs. Ecuador. The Mexican fans reportedly kept the Ecuadorian team up all night. The bigger news out of that game was tragic — three people suffocated during celebrations in Mexico City, and there were also altercations and an unlawful assembly at San Pedro square. It was a sobering topic of conversation before the shift got going.

Anais and I spoke during before the shift started while enjoying sports drinks instead of the water. We were debating Germany's performance in the World Cup.

This was my first six-hour shift, and my first time not on Fan ID hand-out. Eve gave me the choice, and I opted to stick with Floyd's group — four or five of us, teamed up with Deborah. I carried the megaphone while she held the sign for restricted-item storage and ticket prep. Floyd was easygoing, much like Jose on the Fan ID side. The most common question all day was where to find the hospitality entry — Gate 3 — while the vast majority of fans, Bosnian and American alike, moved through Gate 1. Deborah and I were stationed near the security gates by the light rail station, and the "Welcome to the FIFA 2026 World Cup" sign became an unexpected hit — we handed it off to families and kids for photos all afternoon. The megaphone cut out now and then, maybe wind, maybe the battery.

After kickoff we grabbed dinner — veggie option, potato wedges and mushrooms — and I met with Mint, up from Fresno and her buddies. Around the 65–70 minute mark, with USA already up 2-0, we were called back to the gates to help direct the crowd out — much faster than the arrival, but still a big push. We wrapped up around 20:20 PT, and for the first time I waited for the bus without my bike, which took a little longer given the size of the crowd.

Six weeks, six shifts, and a genuinely great experience. Jen has already headed back to Zurich, Eve's off to LA, and there's one more get-together — the volunteer appreciation event on July 6th at PayPal Park. But the shifts themselves are done. Whatever the politics of the day, this was a reminder of what it looks like when people from everywhere come together for something fun and peaceful — a good note to end on.

Trophy Day — Team Photos, the $20 Million Trophy, and One Shift to Go

Backing up a few days first: Saturday, June 27th, I met up with a friend from college at San Pedro Square to watch Portugal vs. Colombia. Even the third screen out there was packed — a good sign of how much the attendance and excitement keep growing now that we're into the Round of 32. It's been tough seeing some big names go this round — Germany, Netherlands, Iran, Turkey, Japan, and a few others are already out of the Cup.

Then today, June 30th, we headed over to the stadium for a team photo event, and the highlight was getting to see the actual World Cup trophy in person — estimated at around $20 million. Staff were understandably careful about keeping everyone at a safe distance, but we still walked away with some really nice shots. All of this is in preparation for the last shift on July 1st, which kicks off at 1:30pm for the six-hour shift (or 1pm for the five-hour shift). This will be my first six-hour shift, assisting the Fan Operations team with wayfinding.

We met at the volunteer center and walked over to the field around noon for group photos with the team. Since it was a Tuesday, not everyone could make it — our volunteers come from Sacramento, Fresno, India, and all over. I spent some time talking with Adrian, the team member who made the trip from Sacramento. He told me about his travels to Germany, his political engagement back home, and how excited he's been to be part of this World Cup — he's also applying for the 2028 LA Olympics.

We also got some shots of the USA vs. Bosnia seating setup — sunny, warm day, and a nice little preview of tomorrow's action ahead of the final game here in Santa Clara on July 1st.

Eve mentioned she'll be heading down to Inglewood after this to support another match on July 10th — still to be determined which teams advance that far. Today also brought a couple of results: Norway beat Ivory Coast, and France beat Sweden. Still ahead of us: Mexico vs. Ecuador, and of course tomorrow's USA vs. Bosnia. We're solidly in the Round of 32 now with the knockout rounds closing in.

On the way back from group photos I caught up with Carlos, who made the drive in from Fremont — pretty conveniently located for these shifts. A good, full day, and the nerves are starting to set in a bit for tomorrow's final shift.

Fifth Shift — PG&E Stories, Croatian Tales, and the Best Fans of the Tournament So Far

Thursday, June 25th was my fifth shift, and at this point it's settling into a comfortable routine. I sat down with Sergio at the volunteer center right around check-in time, an hour ahead of kickoff for Fan ID hand-out at the fence. He's still commuting in all the way from Napa Valley every shift — real dedication. We got into his work as a reviewer and inspector on PG&E lines, which turned into a genuinely fascinating conversation about how critical and demanding that kind of inspection work is. He also talked about the recent Mendocino earthquake and how it affects his inspection workload.

We were also joined by Alex, who came down from Walnut Creek. He's a former referee with Croatian roots, and he's always got great stories from over there — plus some memories from growing up in Germany for a year or two. Between Sergio and Alex, our little corner of the team never runs short on good conversation.

Jose has now led Fan ID hand-out duties for five shifts running, and Jen, our coordinator visiting in from Switzerland, swung by again — everyone has nothing but high marks for her, for Jose, and for Eve. We genuinely know each other as a team at this point, and it shows in how smoothly everything runs.

Fan-wise, today belonged to Australia and Paraguay — both sets of supporters were outstanding. Australia moved through to the Round of 32 and I'm genuinely happy about it; I watched some clips of their team walking into the stadium and it was something else. Ranking the fan bases I've seen come through Santa Clara so far — Australia, Austria, Switzerland, Paraguay, Algeria, Jordan, Turkey — in that rough order, though I'll admit I might be a little biased. There are just so many good teams left that I'm hoping keep advancing.

Fourth Shift — Instagram Spotlights, Off-Shift Watching, and Public Viewing Realities

A whirlwind few days of stadium duty mixed with exploring the local public viewing environments around the South Bay. The biggest highlight from our actual volunteer operational duties for Jordan vs. Algeria came directly from social media — our team group picture made it onto the official FIFA volunteer Instagram updates page! Seeing our local crew highlighted globally was a massive morale boost for everyone on the Fan Operations team.

Outside of my official shifts, the weekend provided an interesting case study on where to watch the games if you don't have a ticket inside the venue. On Saturday, June 20th, I went over to Los Altos to try catching the Germany match at Esther's Cafe. It was a bit akward compared to the last cups when they hosted an incredible turnout in their beer garden; this time, the venue was largely empty and they were playing background music rather than broadcasting the actual live stadium game commentary.

Seeking a more lively atmosphere, a friend and I shifted gears over to Castro Street in Downtown Mountain View to watch at Ludwigs. The vibe there was completely night and day — packed out with supporters, filled with animated conversations, and carrying a great football energy. It is worth noting that the Castro public viewing zone doesn't feature any massive outdoor projection screens like San Pedro Square in San Jose, but the local restaurant crowd made up for it. Still, San Pedro Square Market remains the undisputed number-one spot in the region for a stadium-level experience outside the venue itself.

On Sunday, June 21st, I checked out the Iran vs. Belgium match (which finished in an intense tie) at the International Market near Downtown San Jose on Santa Clara Street by the 101 freeway. The broadcast setup featured an exceptionally high-quality screen and fluid streaming, though the crowd was on the smaller side. The only downside occurred right at the tail end of the first half when the BBQ operator suddenly shut down the main power outlet to the television for five minutes to hook up an appliance.

Third Shift — Turkey vs. Paraguay, Gate B, and a Hyatt Detour Before Kickoff

June 19 started with a smoother commute than usual — bike on the rack of VTA Line 57, an easy combination that saved time and effort. The bus driver recognized the volunteer uniform right away and asked a few questions about how the tournament was going, the kind of small-talk recognition that's become a nice part of these rides.

Before heading to the stadium, I made a stop at the Hyatt Regency, where I'd RSVP'd earlier for a viewing spot to catch some matches ahead of my shift. I left around noon, just as the USA's second group-stage match against Australia was underway — the U.S. already up two goals in the first half. By the time I got to the Hyatt to watch the rest of the half, no further goals came in the second, but the atmosphere in the room stayed lively throughout.

It was a good chance to compare notes between the two viewing spots. The Hyatt has commentators, a bigger crowd of spectators, and staff circulating — more of a celebration-mode energy. The volunteer lounge back at the stadium is quieter by comparison: each volunteer can take up to three bottles of water and today's snack lineup included cashews alongside the usual chips and granola bars.

I caught up with Sergio again, now on his second shift — he's still making the drive in from Napa Valley each time, full commitment. I also met Sue for the first time; she and her husband are volunteering across two different teams, and she's in Fan Operations like me. She was ten years old when England won the World Cup on home soil on July 30, 1966, and her family bought their first color TV just for the tournament. She recently traveled to Istanbul to catch another tournament in person, and while waiting around she ended up supporting Scotland over Morocco. If England and Scotland ever met head-to-head, though, she said she'd back England all the way.

Sergio and I checked in at 15:00 PT, an hour ahead of the shift. We had fewer team members than usual at first, but others trickled in as we went. Eve and Jose got everyone assembled, and we walked out to our usual spot near Gate B. Gates opened sharp at 17:00, three hours ahead of kickoff. From there it was the familiar routine: handing out Fan IDs, explaining the virtual experience, wayfinding, and fielding the steady stream of questions.

Right after kickoff, the team regrouped by the soccer ball sculpture near Gate B — and that's when we heard Paraguay had already scored in the second minute. We took a few more photos on the walk back to the volunteer center. I managed to text my cousin, who'd come down from Vancouver with her husband to watch the game in person. Fans on both sides were great. Turkey held over 70% possession for long stretches and my cousin's family stayed hopeful they'd find a way back into it, but the possession never converted into goals. Paraguay closed it out.

Second Shift — Austria vs. Jordan, Gate B, Fan ID Crew

On June 16, I felt more prepared as I got ready to head to my shift. This time I decided to walk to Westgate Mall — about seven minutes — passing by the new T&T market opening on June 18. No bike today. Bus line 57 was already waiting and nearly empty, one of those frequent runs that goes to the stadium every 10–15 minutes. The ride was quick and comfortable, and I arrived at the volunteer lounge around 14:00 PT, a full three hours before my 17:00 shift start.

The early arrival paid off. The lounge had sound today — official TV was streaming the Norway vs. Iraq match with full audio, a real luxury compared to silent broadcast we had before. It felt like actually being at a stadium. I settled in, watched, and reconnected with people I'd met before: Linda, Sonya, and a new face — Sergio from Napa Valley, sitting front row, clearly a Mexico supporter. He had attended the Switzerland vs. Qatar game as a spectator and this was his first volunteer shift. I gave him a quick rundown of what to expect from Fan ID distribution based on what we did on June 13. He also shared his own story: his first volunteering experience was in St. Petersburg in 2018, where accommodations were covered and the whole setup was quite different. The comparison was interesting — the Bay Area operation is structured differently, but the energy is just as real.

Before we were called up, one of the team leads Nadine informed us that the daily photo challenge results would be announced at the volunteer appreciation event on July 6th — selfies, field shots, the works. I had entered the competition too. Shortly after, Eve Mendez called us up for Fan Operations. She also asked for five volunteers willing to take on a special assignment.

From there, the routine clicked into place. Jose led us on a quick tour of the stadium — always great to walk those concourses when it's still empty and the gates are about to open. We posted up near Gate B, same as before, each of us grabbing a box of Fan IDs. This time we organized the cards by holding them by the card rather than the lanyard — a small fix that made untangling significantly easier and the whole process much smoother.

I was stationed near the VIP entrance again, paired up with Sergio. We had about 40 minutes before gates opened to sort and stage. When the crowd came in, it was heavy — official attendance around 60,000, possibly higher by the time we finished. The Austrian fans were a great crowd, and I got to put my German to work. A group of Austrians asked me in German what I thought the score would be. I told them Austria 3–0 — partly to encourage them, partly just going with the energy. The actual result: Austria 3–1. Not far off. The Jordan fans were just as warm, and I made sure to stay positive with them without giving anything away.

First Shift — Fan ID Crew, Gate B, Switzerland vs. Qatar

The alarm went off well before dawn. I was out the door around 5:00 AM, on my bike, and at the stadium by 6:00 — entering through the back via the San Tomas Aquino Creek Trail to SF Bay Trail. Security waved me through without issue, though I briefly went in the wrong direction and circled back. The ride itself was quieter than expected, with one notable exception: a police action right at the corner of Saratoga Avenue and Williams. Other than that, the streets were empty at that hour — just me and a stillness that wouldn't last long.

Once at the stadium, I met Linda near the fan launch area — she's retired from EMC and now working with the hospitality team. I also reconnected with several teammates from Fan Operations. We assembled as a group, ran through check-in, and at 8:10 AM headed out to our station: Gate B, the VIP line entrance, just as security checks were opening for the day.

Our assignment was Fan ID distribution. Boxes of RFID-embedded cards were waiting for us, exactly as covered in the virtual training sessions. The three of us on the crew set up our table and started handing them out. The opening was quiet — gates had opened on schedule at 9:00, giving us about an hour to organize — but then the surges started. Fans arrived in waves, and the three of us worked steadily to keep the flow moving. The most common question by far: "What's that long line going to the Fan Zone?" The answer was mascots, drums, and an atmosphere building by the minute.

The Fan ID itself is an NFC-enabled card that fans tap on their iPhone or Android to unlock a virtual experience throughout the tournament — match information, interactive content, and upcoming giveaways. Explaining the concept quickly and clearly to hundreds of different people across language barriers is its own kind of skill. The most physically demanding part of the whole shift, though, was untangling the Fan ID lanyards — they come bundled and knotted, and getting them sorted fast while the line builds is genuinely tricky work.

As for the match itself — even before gates fully opened, we caught word that Switzerland had scored off a penalty, and by the time we wrapped distribution and made our way to the crew cafeteria for lunch, Qatar had equalized. We watched the second half from the staff cafeteria — chicken, rice, broccoli, and salad — and caught up with Alex from Walnut Creek and Efren from San Jose, trading notes about the USA's 4–2–1 win against Paraguay the night before. It was exactly the kind of crew energy that makes a long day feel good.

After lunch we walked the concourse, grabbed some photos with Spencer and Jose from the Fan Operations leadership team, and checked in with Eve before the shift wrapped. The final result — Switzerland 1, Qatar 1 — kept things tight. Next up: Tuesday, June 16.

Opening Kickoff — The World Cup Has Arrived

The tournament is officially underway. I arrived at San Pedro Square Market around 10:00 — the opening ceremony was already streaming and the energy was building fast. Early on I took the opportunity to reconnect with a friend from Frankfurt — a quick check-in from a fellow football fan. Then came the Mexico fans. A lot of Mexico fans. The atmosphere at San Pedro Square was vibrant in the best possible way — it felt like the city actually showed up.

It was hot. 35°C — that's 95°F — and barely a cloud in the sky. No shade-seeking here, just full sun and the kind of heat that makes cold drinks disappear fast. Nobody seemed to mind.

The crowd wasn't just fans either. Reporters were everywhere including Swiss TV (I think). Mayor Matt Mahan was there, I shook his hand while he was walking with Zaileen "Z" Janmohamed, the Bay Area Host Committee CEO, to the stage. News anchors, cameras, official-looking lanyards. There was a real sense that this was a civic moment, not just a watch party.

The match result — a 2–0 victory — drew some pointed commentary from the German press. But from where I was standing, surrounded by supporters who've been waiting years for this, the final score felt almost secondary. This was a celebration. The fans were happy. The World Cup is here.

Pre-Tournament 2
Sunday, June 7, 2026 📍 San Francisco Bay Area Stadium, Santa Clara, CA

Fan Operations Training — Inside the Venue, Less Than a Week to Go

<

I arrived well before the 0930 scheduled start — early enough to mingle before the room filled up. One of the first people I met works in project management in the financial sector, lives nearby, and is also thinking about biking to the same light rail station to commute to the stadium for the matches. A good reminder of how wide this volunteer network runs — fan operations, media, catering, broadcasting, all converging in the same building.

The training opened with some striking numbers: over a million people applied to volunteer for FIFA World Cup 2026. In the Bay Area alone, roughly 1,000 were selected. The team lead, who has been here building this out for a full year, set the tone simply: stay positive, work as a team. It's a large, layered operation with FIFA staff, multiple functional areas, and thousands of additional stadium personnel.

The day covered a lot of ground — security awareness, the fan rulebook, Rules of Play, and the two shift structures. The fan operation center will be open before shifts and we're welcome to stay at the volunteer center after, but accreditation passes are strictly for shift time only. The team was clear on that.

One important note for June 13th: it's expected to be in the high 80s °F / 30 °D. The team stressed this repeatedly — wear the hat, apply sunscreen, drink water. Hydration breaks and a 45-minute meal break are built into every shift. The Clipper cards for public transit will activate on each match day.

The group today came from everywhere in the Bay and across the US. Someone in the room had volunteered at the 1998 World Cup in France. Volunteers who drove hours or took long train rides just to be here. About 40–60 people in fan operations alone. The venue was still being set up around us — which somehow made it feel even more real. Six days to go.
